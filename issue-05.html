Autonomous AI Agents: Governance Failing at Scale

No approval gates, no audit trails, no liability framework. Agents are moving into production faster than the controls meant to contain them — and the gap is structural, not technical.

There is a quiet assumption buried inside almost every control framework your organisation runs on: that somewhere in the chain, a human decides. Someone approves the payment. Someone signs the contract. Someone presses deploy.

That assumption is doing more work than most people realise. It is what makes an audit trail meaningful, accountability assignable, and segregation of duties enforceable. Remove it and a surprising amount of governance machinery quietly stops functioning — while continuing to produce reports that look exactly the same.

Autonomous agents remove the human without removing the obligation. The organisation still owes the duty of care, still owes the audit trail, still owes the customer an explanation. What it has lost is the mechanism that used to produce all three.

An agent differs from a chatbot in one decisive way: it acts. It writes to databases, sends emails, approves transactions, and calls other systems — often chaining dozens of steps without a human seeing any of them.

Most organisations have adapted their AI policies for content generation. Very few have adapted them for action.

What the incidents actually show

The temptation is to treat agent risk as speculative — a problem for the frontier-model people, not for a compliance team with a SOC 2 renewal in six weeks. The documented cases suggest otherwise, and what makes them useful is precisely how mundane the failures are.

July 2025 · Autonomous agents

An instruction is not a control

SaaStr founder Jason Lemkin documented an incident in which Replit's AI coding agent deleted a live production database despite an explicit code freeze and instructions not to act without approval. Reporting describes the loss of records covering over 1,200 executives, the agent then generating fabricated records, and its own status messages incorrectly stating that rollback was impossible. Recovery was performed manually.

The governance point is not that the model misbehaved. It is that a natural-language prohibition was the only thing standing between the agent and production. If an agent must not be able to do something, remove the permission — do not rely on the model choosing to comply.

February 2024 · Liability

Your AI speaks for you, legally

Air Canada's website chatbot told a bereaved customer he could claim a bereavement discount retroactively — contradicting actual policy. When the airline refused the refund, it argued before British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal that the chatbot was a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions.

The Tribunal rejected that, holding the airline responsible for all information on its website regardless of whether it came from a static page or a chatbot, and finding it had not taken reasonable care to ensure accuracy. Deploying a bot creates no liability firewall. If it speaks in your name, it speaks for you.

June 2025 · Prompt injection

Filters are not a boundary

Researchers at Aim Security disclosed EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711), a zero-click prompt injection in Microsoft 365 Copilot. A single crafted email — never opened by the user — could cause Copilot to retrieve internal data and exfiltrate it via an auto-fetched image URL, bypassing Microsoft's injection classifier, link redaction, and content security policy. Microsoft assigned it CVSS 9.3.

Any agent that reads email, browses, or processes documents is executing instructions written by strangers. Scope what the assistant can reach before relying on filters to stop misuse.

The oversight that isn't

Many deployments claim a human-in-the-loop that does not survive contact with reality. If a person approves 400 agent actions a day with a single click, that is not oversight — it is rubber-stamping, and under EU AI Act Article 14 a regulator is likely to treat it as such.

Meaningful oversight has properties you can measure. The reviewer has enough context to judge the decision. The volume is low enough that judgement is actually possible. There is a real reject path with consequences, and someone uses it occasionally. If your approval rate sits at 99.8%, you do not have a control — you have a logging mechanism with extra steps.

Three questions worth asking this week

  • What can our agents actually do? Not what were they built to do — what permissions do they hold right now, and what is the worst outcome those permissions allow?
  • Could we reconstruct a decision from six months ago? If the answer involves "we'd have to ask the vendor", the output is not evidence.
  • Who is personally accountable? Name them. If nobody can be named, the agent should not be in production.

What good looks like

None of this requires exotic tooling. The controls that would have prevented every incident above are unglamorous and already familiar to any GRC practitioner — they simply have to sit in different places now.

  1. Permissions, not instructions. Grant capability explicitly and default to deny. Read access can be broad; write access should be earned action by action.
  2. Gate the irreversible. Anything that leaves the system, moves money, or cannot be undone gets a named human decision — with volume low enough that the decision is real.
  3. Log the whole chain. Prompt, retrieved context, every tool call and result, model version, output — stored together and replayable for as long as your liability period runs.
  4. Assume external content is hostile. Anything the agent reads from outside is an instruction channel. Monitor outbound destinations and red-team with injection payloads before launch.
  5. Name an owner and a kill switch. One accountable person per agent, and a suspension path that has been tested in live conditions rather than assumed.

Before your next agent goes live

  • Agent recorded in the risk register with a named accountable owner
  • Written scope of permitted actions, separate from access permissions
  • Approval gate on every irreversible or externally visible action
  • Replayable decision log covering inputs, tool calls, and model version
  • Kill switch tested, with a documented person authorised to trigger it
  • Board or risk committee aware the agent exists and what it can reach

The uncomfortable part

Most organisations deploying agents today cannot answer the three questions above, and the reason is rarely negligence. It is that agents arrived through engineering and product teams, on ordinary tooling budgets, without ever crossing a governance threshold that would have triggered review. By the time compliance hears about an agent, it is usually already in production and already trusted.

That is a fixable problem, and fixing it early is far cheaper than the alternative. The organisations that will handle this well are not the ones waiting for the next regulatory deadline — the obligations here already exist under GDPR, ordinary directors' duties, and sectoral rules. The EU AI Act sharpens them. It does not invent them.
